Modelling Length of Stay
in the Intensive Care Unit

Daniel Rodrigues

Goal: model ICU length of stay and, above all, justify every modelling decision: the type of regression, how I chose the variables, the interpretation and the assumptions.

The data & the problem

300 patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery.

We model the length of stay in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), the number of days a patient spends in the ward for critically ill patients.

Dependent variable: ICU length of stay, in days

Candidate explanatory variables

  • EuroSCORE, European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (pre-operative risk score)
  • Type of surgery, bypass (coronary artery bypass graft), valve, or both
  • Atrial fibrillation, irregular heart rhythm (yes / no)
  • Age and sex
  • Diastolic / systolic blood pressure, in mmHg (millimetres of mercury)
Seven candidate predictors. The dependent variable is length of stay in days. Note up front that the data set has no discharge/death indicator, only these nine columns, which matters for the model choice two slides from now.

Part 1

Methods

Choosing the regression · data quality · variable selection

Choosing the model

The outcome is a continuous, positive, skewed duration (days). Among the methods in the course:

✗ Survival analysis (Cox)

No discharge/death indicator in the data → censoring cannot be defined, so time-to-event modelling is not possible here.

✓ Linear regression on log(stay)

Primary model. Handles the skew and is interpretable: the “log-linear regression” of the syllabus.

✓ Poisson GLM (log link)

Cross-check. Treat the stay as a count of days → fit the course GLM and compare (mean = variance is a caveat).

Primary: linear regression on log(stay); cross-checked with a Poisson GLM (log link).

This answers the examiner's first question, "why not survival analysis for a length of stay?" Answer: there is no event or censoring indicator in the dataset, so a duration can't be treated as time-to-event. Poisson regression IS usable if we treat the stay as a count of days, we keep it as a GLM cross-check (shown later) and compare it with the primary log-linear model.

The outcome → take logs

ICU length of stay is strongly right-skewed (median 1.5, max 29.3 days). The natural logarithm removes the skew → we model log(length of stay).

Figure 1, raw scale (left) vs natural-log scale (right). Modelling the log is the “log-linear regression” of the syllabus.

The raw histogram has a long right tail; on the log scale it is roughly symmetric. This is the evidence behind the previous decision. Note the spike of short stays, it returns as a "floor" in the residuals later.

Data quality: impossible values

Inspecting the variables for data-entry errors, using simple physiological limits (systolic 50–250, diastolic 20–150 mmHg; age and EuroSCORE were also checked and are fine), flags four impossible blood-pressure values:

Systolic pressure

1911.9 and 18.7 mmHg (patients 25, 269)

Diastolic pressure

8.7 and 11.0 mmHg (patients 223, 228)

Set to missing → 296 patients with complete data. A sensitivity analysis later confirms the conclusions do not depend on this step.

A systolic pressure of 1911 mmHg is physically impossible, clearly a typo. I set the four impossible values to missing rather than guessing corrected values.

Why not the raw scale?

Linear regression on the untransformed stay: residuals fan out as predictions grow (spread not constant) and are right-skewed.

Figure 2, residual analysis, raw length of stay. Two assumptions violated → the raw scale is inadequate.

This justifies the transformation empirically: the raw model breaks the equal-spread and normality assumptions. After taking logs (shown on the assumptions slide) both improve.

Choosing the variables

Start with all seven variables, then simplify using course tools:

  • Only EuroSCORE, diastolic & systolic pressure are significant
  • Nested-model test (anova): dropping age, sex, atrial fibrillation, surgery gives p = 0.72 → they add nothing here
  • Lower AIC / BIC and higher adjusted R² confirm the smaller model

AIC = Akaike, BIC = Bayesian Information Criterion (lower is better).

Why this makes clinical sense: the EuroSCORE already incorporates age, sex and the type of surgery, so once it is in the model these variables add no independent information.

Key clinical point: the EuroSCORE is a composite risk score that already includes age, sex and the type/complexity of surgery as components, so it is no surprise those variables drop out, their information is already captured. "Not significant" is also not the same as "no effect": atrial fibrillation is not a EuroSCORE component, and with only 29 AFIB patients the power is limited (see Limitations).

Fitting the model: least squares

The coefficients are calculated so that the sum of the squared distances from the observations to the line is minimised, the least-squares method.

log(ICU stay) = b0 + b1 · EuroSCORE + b2 · diastolic + b3 · systolic

Slopes (b1, b2, b3)

The change in log(stay) for a one-unit increase in each predictor, holding the others fixed.

Intercept (b0)

The predicted log(stay) when all predictors are zero. It anchors the line (which passes through the mean of the data); "all zero" is not a real patient, so it is not interpreted on its own.

Least squares produces the four fitted numbers shown next.

This is the "how do we get the numbers" slide. Least squares (in STATS_1) picks the coefficients that minimise the sum of squared residuals. All four are estimated together; the intercept is not chosen, it falls out of the fit and equals mean(log stay) minus each slope times that predictor's mean, so the line passes through the average point. We do not interpret it clinically because all-predictors-zero is not a real patient.

What the coefficients mean

Coefficients are on the log scale → exponentiate to read each as a % change in the expected stay per one-unit increase (other variables fixed).

Each extra EuroSCORE point → about 7% longer expected ICU stay, the dominant factor. Higher blood pressure means modestly longer stays.

On the log scale the model describes the geometric mean, the typical patient.

Read effects as multiplicative. EuroSCORE dominates and is clinically sensible. The blood-pressure effects are small; I'll caution about their interpretation in Limitations (timing not recorded).

Honest framing: a formal normality test (Shapiro–Wilk) is still significant, so residuals are not perfectly normal, but at n=296 that test is very sensitive and the estimates are robust. The diagonal lower boundary is the floor effect from the minimum stay; I list it as a limitation.

A generalized linear model: Poisson

A GLM links the predictors to the outcome through a link function. The course's GLMs are logistic (logit) and Poisson (log link). Treating the stay as a count of days, we fit a Poisson GLM and read exp(coef) as rate ratios.

Same direction, similar size → consistent.

Assumption check. Poisson assumes mean = variance, but the days are overdispersed (variance 13 ≫ mean 2.7; overdispersion ≈ 2.2) → its p-values are optimistic. The log-linear model needs no such assumption and remains the primary model.

The GLM lecture distinguishes a log LINK from a log TRANSFORMATION: Poisson models ln(E[days]) while the log-linear model models E[ln days]. Both give multiplicative effects, and here they agree, which strengthens the conclusion. But the Poisson mean=variance assumption fails (overdispersion ≈ 2.2), so I keep the log-linear model as primary and present Poisson as a cross-check.

Limitations

  • Modest explanatory power, adjusted R² ≈ 0.20; most of the variation in ICU stay is driven by factors not in the data.
  • Floor / routine-stay structure, a spike of short ~1-day stays leaves a lower boundary in the residuals; one linear model can't fully separate “routine” from “complicated”.
  • Small subgroups, only 29 patients with atrial fibrillation; “not significant” may reflect limited power, not absence of effect.
  • Undocumented details, blood-pressure timing and the exact EuroSCORE version are unknown, so those effects need cautious interpretation.
  • In-sample only, no external validation of predictive performance.
  • Poisson GLM overdispersion, the cross-check model breaks the mean = variance assumption (≈ 2.2), so it is supportive evidence only, not the primary model.
Putting limitations on a slide shows critical thinking and pre-empts the hard questions: low R², the floor effect, underpowered subgroups, missing metadata, and no validation.

Robustness & conclusions

Sensitivity check, all 300 patients, no cleaning

  • EuroSCORE & diastolic pressure stay strongly significant → result robust
  • Systolic pressure loses significance, distorted by the 1911.9 mmHg error → shows why cleaning mattered

Conclusion. Longer ICU stay is associated with higher operative risk (EuroSCORE) and higher blood pressure, via a log-linear regression, and a Poisson GLM agrees. The model is statistically significant but explains only a modest share of the variation.

Deliberately say "associated with", not "well described", the associations are real and significant, but R² is only 0.20. End by reiterating that each decision was data-driven and course-aligned.
