The Grand Challenge · Invisible Context

When health decisions fail, how much of the relevant context was never digitally or cognitively visible in the first place?

Three labs of the EFMI LEAD cohort took on this question within One Digital Health (ODH): one diagnoses what stays invisible, one designs how BMHI could make it visible, and one translates the result into European action.

The Grand Challenge · Invisible Context

Context that never reaches the decision

The question is not whether context matters, which is well established, but how much relevant context was never made visible, and what kind of absence each gap is.

Five different kinds of absence

1Informational: a determinant that is never recorded

2Semantic: a determinant recorded in a form other systems cannot interpret

3Experiential: a determinant for which no structured field of suitable shape exists

4Governance: a determinant held in another sector’s well-governed database

5Evaluative: a determinant that no outcome indicator measures

Treating every gap as “missing data” keeps the problem in place, because each kind needs a different remedy and a different institutional actor.

In each case the clinical reasoning is sound. The decision still fails, because the determining context was neither digitally available nor cognitively present when it was made.

Alizadeh et al. [1]; Benis et al. [2]; Champion et al. [3]

IFAL · Five layers

Five layers of invisibility

Each layer describes a different reason why a determinant is not available at the point of decision, and indicates which actor would be in a position to address it. Layers can overlap; the most persistent cases involve more than one.

1 · INFORMATIONAL

Is it recorded at all?

No practical way exists to record it.

2 · SEMANTIC

Can other systems interpret it?

It is recorded, but not in a standard, computable form.

3 · EXPERIENTIAL

Is there a suitable field for it?

Lived experience does not fit structured fields.

4 · GOVERNANCE

Can it cross the sector boundary?

The data exist, but in another sector’s system.

5 · EVALUATIVE

Is it measured?

No outcome indicator includes it.

How the layers reinforce each other

Invisible context is therefore a property of the information ecosystem as a whole, not a collection of isolated missing data points.

Patients and families

The burden of missing context is concentrated among groups that are already disadvantaged.

Clinicians

Institutions

Institutions absorb the consequences as costs that are not attributed to missing context.

Ecosystem

BVDL · Overview

Available is not the same as visible

A determinant becomes visible only when it passes through every step below. Visibility therefore needs an end-to-end infrastructure, from data acquisition to action.

1 · Captured

The determinant enters a digital system.

→

2 · Represented

It is recorded in a defined, computable form.

→

3 · Exchanged

It can travel between systems and sectors.

→

4 · Interpreted

The receiving system understands what it means.

→

5 · Acted on

It reaches the decision it should inform.

The challenge is not the absence of data, but the absence of architectures that turn scattered contextual signals into actionable intelligence.

Upstream of the clinical encounter

Health institutions, environmental agencies, social services, schools and public-order authorities all hold part of the picture, but traditional health systems rarely integrate their data.

Denecke et al. [7]; Kelly & Fussell [10]; Harrison & Dean [11]

BVDL · Visibility responses

From diagnosis to visibility design

Each layer of invisibility names a different failure, so each needs a different BMHI response.

InformationalOpen new data-acquisition channels: sensors, citizen-generated data, environmental monitoring, public information systems and structured collection instruments.
SemanticApply common terminologies, ontologies and interoperability standards such as SNOMED CT, LOINC and FHIR.
ExperientialTurn narratives, patient-reported outcomes and social context into computable information, using structured forms and AI-assisted extraction.
GovernanceEstablish the legal, organisational and technical mechanisms that allow data to be exchanged across sectors.
EvaluativeCreate indicators that measure contextual determinants and include them in routine monitoring frameworks.

The hardest layers to build for. Informational and experiential invisibility usually require new data items in health information systems, and therefore new input mechanisms. AI tools such as NLP and LLMs could reduce that burden, but their reliability requires continuous professional oversight.

Visibility interventions for the five archetypes

Proposed visibility interventionExpected outcome
1Structured SDOH fields, citizen-reported data, and linkage with social-service information systemsSocial determinants become available for care planning, population monitoring and service coordination.
2Environmental sensor networks, monitoring platforms and geospatial linkageEnvironmental exposures can be connected to individual, population, animal and ecosystem health signals.

These interventions depend on interoperable architectures that keep meaning, provenance, quality and trust across organisational and sector boundaries.

BVDL · Overview and ODHness

Three kinds of interoperability, and ODHness

If any one of them fails, invisibility returns, even when data capture works.

SHARED MEANING

Semantic interoperability

Controlled terminologies and ontologies ensure that a hospital, a veterinary clinic and an environmental agency read the same indicator in the same way.

DATA EXCHANGE

Technical interoperability

Protocols such as HL7 FHIR and RESTful APIs, with secure authorisation, let legacy health systems and IoT environmental sensors exchange data.

TRUSTED USE

Governance interoperability

Process standards define how organisations collaborate, govern access and trigger coordinated responses.

ODHness as a visibility metric. ODHness is the degree to which a digital ecosystem integrates human, animal and environmental data into a coherent framework for health intelligence. The greater a system’s ability to identify, connect and act on invisible determinants across domains, the higher its ODHness.

BVDL · Issues and research

Implementation issues, turned into research

Making context visible creates its own problems. BVDL treats each one as a researchable question, with a study design and the partners it would need.

PRIVACY AND DATA PROTECTION

Invisible health inference without awareness

Health determinants may become visible to institutions before they become visible to the people they describe.

RESPONSIBILITY

Invisible accountability gaps

When a health-relevant conclusion comes from several connected systems, responsibility for an error can become invisible.

RESISTANCE TO CHANGE

Invisible determinants of organisational adoption

Deeper causes stay hidden in organisational culture, informal workflows, professional identity and perceived threats to autonomy.

SYSTEM REDUNDANCY

Useful versus harmful redundancy

It is unclear when overlapping datasets improve performance and when they only add complexity, cost and burden.

The greatest barriers to implementing ODH may not be visible technical limits but invisible interactions between data, institutions, technologies, governance structures and human behaviour.

European Translation Lab

ETL · Strategic proposal engine

Why is this strategically European? ETL argues that Invisible Context is not a local shortfall but a structural feature of European health systems, measurable, shared and cross-border, and it maps the European routes to act on it.

Konstantinos Karitis · Janne Kommusaar · Maria Parra Rodriguez-Armijo · with thanks to Josefin Hagström

ETL · Why Europe

A structural gap, reproduced across Europe

European by nature

Structural, not local

The gap recurs in every European health system, whatever its funding model, language or geography.

Determinants cross borders

People migrate, pollution spreads and diseases pass between animals and people.

Workforce beyond national capacity

WHO warns of a growing health-worker shortage alongside a widening digital-skills gap.

A window that is open now

Fairer health

Neither EU health-equity objectives nor the UN Sustainable Development Goals can be met while social and environmental context stays invisible.

Health AI governance

The AI Act classifies most medical AI as high-risk and requires high-quality, representative data with human oversight.

The European Health Data Space

A one-time opportunity to include standardised social and environmental context in routine health data across member states.

ETL · Two gates

ETL’s two gates

ETL connects the cohort’s loop: it receives one question from BVDL and passes another to IFAL.

FROM BVDL

“Is the strategic relevance compatible with implementable digital infrastructures?”

ETL asks itself six questions from the European side, and names what it needs from BVDL for each.

TO IFAL

“Which invisibilities are large enough to justify European collaboration?”

  1. Health impact
  2. Persistence across systems
  3. Cross-sector or cross-border character
  4. BMHI tractability
  5. ODH relevance
  6. European added value

Not every invisibility becomes a European Grand Challenge. IFAL then returns each invisibility as confirmed, reframed or deprioritised. These criteria have not yet been applied to the five archetypes; that is the task of IFAL’s next iteration.
