IFAL · The diagnostic lens

Five layers of invisibility

1 · INFORMATIONAL

Is it recorded at all?

No practical, low-effort way exists to capture it.

The absence of data is read as absence of relevance.

2 · SEMANTIC

Can other systems interpret it?

Recorded as free text or a local checkbox, without a standard code.

It cannot be exchanged, used by decision support or aggregated.

3 · EXPERIENTIAL

Is there a suitable field for it?

Lived experience, preferences and informal care fit no structured field.

Factors behind adherence and outcomes are missing from the decision.

4 · GOVERNANCE

Can it cross the sector boundary?

The data exist, but in another sector’s system, with no legal or organisational route.

Shared learning across sectors and species does not take place.

5 · EVALUATIVE

Is it measured?

No outcome indicator that guides institutional behaviour includes it.

What is not measured is not requested, so the gap persists.

Grand Challenge document, Table 1 · Sood et al. [4]; Benis et al. [2]; Chidambaram et al. [5]; Alizadeh et al. [1]; Champion et al. [3]; Fraser et al. [6]

Four mechanisms of persistence

Capture friction

Informational

No suitable structured field, screening routine or low-effort pathway. Perceived importance does not lead to consistent documentation.

Semantic fragmentation

Semantic

Free text, local categories and heterogeneous formats that cannot be reliably interpreted, exchanged or reused across settings.

Representational exclusion

SemanticEvaluative

Devices and algorithms validated on populations that differ from those they serve; data poverty reinforces existing inequities.

Boundary and incentive failures

ExperientialGovernanceEvaluative

Experience stays in conversation; veterinary and environmental data stay in separate systems; missing indicators keep both out of decisions.

Archetype 1 · Social circumstances not recorded

SDOH Documentation Gap

Housing, food security and transport affect outcomes, but are rarely documented in a structured way. The gap reflects the structure of the record more than a lack of clinical awareness.

18.2%

consistently document housing instability

16.4%

consistently document food insecurity

22.7%

consistently document transport access

14.8×

higher odds of documenting a social vs. an environmental determinant

Percentages: among providers who consider the determinant clinically vital.

Decisions that fail

Discharge without housing information

Housing insecurity, which predicts the readmission, is not in the chart.

Insulin regimen without food-security information

Designed around regular meals for a food-insecure patient.

Follow-up without transport information

Missed visits are recorded as non-compliance, not as a problem of access.

Informational · no structured field, ease of charting drives captureSemantic · free text, no standard codes
Sood et al., 2024 [4] · Smoking status, the one social determinant with a long-established structured field, is documented consistently.

Archetype 2 · Environmental exposures not recorded

Environmental Determinant Blindness

Air pollution, water contamination, household toxins and extreme weather are seldom in clinical records or in the indicators by which institutions are assessed.

<5%

of providers consistently document any environmental determinant

31%

document infectious-disease outbreaks, the only exception

18.4%

find environmental determinants easy to chart (social: 68.2%)

Evaluative: readmissions, mortality and length of stay are not adjusted for exposure, so even captured data would not reach the metrics, and there is little demand for them.

Decisions that fail

Paediatric asthma triage without air-quality data

Triage on clinical signs alone; air quality where the child lives is not in the record.

Perinatal counselling without exposure history

No history of water contamination or household toxin exposure.

Informational: exposure data sit in environmental-monitoring systems, with no routine route into the clinical record.

Sood et al., 2024 [4]; Benis et al., 2021 [2]

Archetype 3 · Groups under-represented in device and algorithm data

Algorithmic Data Poverty

Devices and algorithms developed on some populations perform less accurately for others. Patients are present in the encounter but misrepresented in the model, and this is rarely measured after deployment.

11–19%

lower accuracy of recognition systems for Black women

Pulse oximetry overestimates oxygen saturation in darker skin pigmentation

Semantic · absent from training data or treated as an outlierEvaluative · subgroup performance rarely reported

Decisions that fail

Delayed escalation

COVID-19 treatment is escalated late because pulse oximetry overestimates saturation.

AI-assisted imaging

Performs less well for groups under-represented in the training data.

Video-first telemedicine triage

Excludes patients with hearing or visual impairment.

The issue is not clinical judgement: the patient is misrepresented in the data on which the system was developed.

Chidambaram et al., 2024 [5]; Fraser et al., 2023 [6]

Archetype 4 · Patient preferences and care context not captured

Patient Narrative Absence

Treatment preferences, lived experience and informal care arrangements have no suitable structured field, and are often lost at handovers between care settings.

Experiential: unlike the SDOH gap, the issue is not the absence of a field but the absence of a field of suitable form. Free text exists, but cannot be retrieved reliably at decision time.

  The evidence is qualitative: loss of patient narrative drives unsafe and inequitable decisions. Systems modelling describes it as an unlinked decision in the continuity of care.

Decisions that fail

Escalation contrary to an unrecorded preference

Ventilation is escalated for a patient who had asked for comfort-only care months earlier, in an outpatient visit.

Care plan that omits the informal caregiver

A heart-failure plan assumes self-management; the patient depends on a daughter with two jobs, documented nowhere.

Determinants that stay unseen

Alizadeh et al., 2025 [1]; Champion et al., 2019 [3]

Archetype 5 · Data held in other sectors

Cross-Sector Governance Silos

Here the data are not missing. They exist, are structured and are maintained in veterinary, agricultural, environmental or foreign health systems.

Governance: what is missing is the legal, organisational and technical route that would let them reach the clinical decision.

During COVID-19, fragmented surveillance data meant public-health decisions were based on partial information. Ecosystem-scale signals remain outside the clinical data-capture surface.

Decisions that fail

  Animal-health signals

Livestock mortality and wildlife die-offs never reach human public-health decision-makers.

  Immunisation held in another member state

A migrant patient’s history is unavailable to the treating clinician.

  Environmental alert

A regional water-contamination event generates no alert in local primary care.

Benis et al., 2021 [2]; Alizadeh et al., 2025 [1]

IFAL · To whom is it invisible?

Who carries the burden

  Patients and families

Concentrated among groups already disadvantaged.

  • Low-income, food-insecure households
  • Rural and transport-poor areas
  • Recent immigrants and refugees
  • Residents of polluted, climate-vulnerable areas
  • People with sensory, motor or cognitive impairment
  • Patients outside validation populations
  • Family and informal caregivers

  Clinicians

Deciding with information they know is incomplete.

  • Primary-care providers
  • Discharge planners and case managers
  • Pulmonology, paediatrics, occupational medicine
  • Users of AI tools with no way to detect bias
  • Palliative, psychiatry and paediatric teams
  • Social and community health workers
  • One Health practitioners and veterinarians

  Institutions

Absorb costs never attributed to missing context.

  • Safety-net hospitals (readmissions)
  • Payers under value-based contracts
  • Public-health and environmental agencies
  • Device and AI makers, regulators
  • Veterinary surveillance and social services
  • Cross-border health-information exchanges

  Ecosystem

Knowledge kept in parallel to clinical care.

  • The One Health community
  • Environmental epidemiologists and climate-health researchers
  • Communities whose social data never aggregate
  • Patient experience that could inform population decisions

BVDL · Defining the target

What “visible” means for BMHI

Captured

The determinant enters a digital system.

Represented

Recorded in a defined, computable form.

Exchanged

It can travel between systems and sectors.

Interpreted

The receiving system understands it.

Acted on

It reaches the decision it should inform.

Two families of determinants to make visible

  Environmental determinants

Air and water quality, hazardous sites, climate. Chronic low-level exposures very rarely link back to individual patient records.

  Social determinants

Housing, income, education, safety: the conditions of daily life. In routine clinical workflows they mostly remain background noise.

BVDL’s task: define the technical, operational and semantic possibilities to turn hidden determinants into visible, actionable contextual intelligence.

Kirtchuk & Wylie, 2021 [9]

One BMHI response per layer

InformationalOpen new data-acquisition channels: sensors, citizen-generated data, environmental monitoring, public information systems, structured collection instruments.
SemanticApply common terminologies, ontologies and interoperability standards such as SNOMED CT, LOINC and FHIR.
ExperientialTurn narratives, patient-reported outcomes and social context into computable information, with structured forms and AI-assisted extraction.
GovernanceEstablish the legal, organisational and technical mechanisms that let data be exchanged across sectors.
EvaluativeCreate indicators that measure contextual determinants and include them in routine monitoring frameworks.

The hardest layers to build for

Informational and experiential gaps need new input mechanisms, which add work for staff. NLP and LLMs could reduce it, under continuous professional oversight.

Ontologies as a cross-cutting answer

SNOMED CT has added social-determinant concepts, but many environmental and social concepts are missing. Ontology work must be inclusive, or it creates new governance invisibility.

BVDL · Implementing the ODH paradigm

Three kinds of interoperability

SHARED MEANING

Semantic

SNOMED CT, LOINC and environmental vocabularies make a hospital, a veterinary clinic and an environmental agency read the same indicator the same way.

DATA EXCHANGE

Technical

HL7 FHIR and RESTful APIs with secure authorisation (OAuth 2.0) let legacy health systems and IoT sensors exchange data without point-to-point interfaces.

TRUSTED USE

Governance

Process standards for collaboration, access and coordinated response, such as a joint alert from livestock monitoring to public-health officials.

Prerequisites

A common digital architecture: APIs, data models, shared identifiers, terminology services.

Provenance and quality: assurance, entity resolution and linkage across data made for different purposes.

Scalable infrastructure: near-real-time ingestion, distributed processing, AI where appropriate.

Visibility becomes a property of the whole ecosystem, not of any single information system.

Bodenreider et al. [17]; WHO and HL7 [15, 16]; Vorisek et al. [18]; WHO [12, 19, 20]

BVDL · A proposed metric

ODHness: measuring visibility maturity

The degree to which a digital ecosystem integrates human, animal and environmental data into one coherent framework for health intelligence. The better a system can identify, connect and act on invisible determinants across domains, the higher its ODHness.

  Digital humanities

Cultural, behavioural, educational and social data alongside clinical information, so the social context of health trajectories becomes visible.

  Digital animalities

Sensors, tracking, genomics and veterinary platforms to detect zoonotic transmission and antimicrobial resistance, where many infections begin.

  Digital environmentalities

Observing and modelling environmental conditions that affect humans and animals, linking stressors to health as the climate changes.

ODHness also reflects governance: standards, ethical data sharing and cross-sector collaboration. Higher ODHness signals readiness for prevention, preparedness and resilience.

Marmot et al. [23]; Anastasiadou et al. [24]; Mackenzie & Jeggo [25]; Destoumieux-Garzón et al. [26]; Jones et al. [27]; Landrigan et al. [28]; Whitmee et al. [29]; Romanello et al. [30]

BVDL · From implementation issues to research

Four issues, four research challenges

PRIVACY AND DATA PROTECTION

Invisible health inference without awareness

Can integrated non-health data predict clinical risk, and when does that exceed what citizens expect? Multi-source linkage study plus acceptability.

RESPONSIBILITY

Invisible accountability gaps

Where do accountability gaps emerge in multi-sector data ecosystems, and which governance keeps decisions traceable? Comparative governance case study.

RESISTANCE TO CHANGE

Invisible determinants of adoption

Which hidden organisational factors drive adoption or rejection of ODH data integration? Mixed-methods study during a pilot.

SYSTEM REDUNDANCY

Useful versus harmful redundancy

Which overlapping data sources add resilience and accuracy, and which only add cost and burden? Comparative modelling study.

The greatest barriers to ODH may not be visible technical limits but invisible interactions between data, institutions, technologies, governance and human behaviour.

ETL · The rationale

Why Europe, and why now

Why Europe

Structural, not local

The gap recurs in every European health system; income-related health inequalities persist in all 27 member states.

Determinants cross borders

People migrate, pollution spreads, diseases pass between animals and people; EU cross-border health-threats law recognises this.

Workforce beyond national capacity

WHO warns of a health-worker shortage and a widening digital-skills gap.

Why now

Fairer health

Neither EU equity objectives nor the UN SDGs can be met while social and environmental context stays invisible.

Health AI governance

The AI Act treats most medical AI as high-risk, requiring representative data and human oversight.

The European Health Data Space

A one-time opportunity to include standardised social and environmental context in routine health data.

OECD/EU [62]; Reg. (EU) 2022/2371 [63]; WHO Europe [64, 65]; UN [66]; AI Act [67]; EHDS, Reg. (EU) 2025/327 [69]
