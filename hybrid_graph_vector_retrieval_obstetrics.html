Good morning. I am Daniel Rodrigues, from the HEADS programme. The problem

Fluent answers are not the same as trustworthy answers.

LLMs are fluent, but not yet trustworthy for the clinic

Fluent and fast, but they hallucinate facts and citations with no source to check. The evidence is piling up:

Clinical decision support still struggles to reason over guidelines that are long and sometimes contradictory. Large language models look like an answer: fluent, fast, good summarisers. The problem is trust, and the evidence is now substantial. In a survey across fifteen specialties, over 90 percent of clinicians had already encountered a medical hallucination, and 85 percent thought it could harm a patient. A physician-led study of nearly 900 chatbot answers to real patient questions found up to 13 percent were unsafe. Under adversarial clinical prompts, hallucination runs from 50 to over 80 percent; only about a third of AI-generated medical citations are fully accurate; and a recent evaluation found ChatGPT Health under-triaged about half of emergencies. So the question is: how do we keep the fluency but add grounding, traceability, and safety?

Hallucination, concretely

Clinician

What is the maintenance dose of magnesium sulphate for eclampsia prophylaxis?

Plain LLM

The maintenance dose is 2 g/hour by IV infusion for 24 hours (see NICE NG133, Table 4.2).

Three things just went wrong

  • Wrong number. The dose should be 1 g/hour, not 2.
  • Invented citation. That table reference does not exist.
  • No way to tell. It sounds fluent and confident, so the error hides in plain sight.

We need answers that are grounded in a real source and show that source.

Let me make hallucination concrete. A clinician asks a simple, common question: the maintenance dose of magnesium sulphate for eclampsia prophylaxis. A plain language model answers fluently, and it is wrong in two ways at once. The dose it gives is double the correct one, and the citation it points to does not exist. The dangerous part is the third point: it reads as confident and well referenced, so unless you already know the answer, you cannot tell it is wrong. This is exactly why we need answers that are grounded in a real source and that show that source, so the clinician can check.

There is a second, subtler problem. Obstetric care is governed by several respected bodies: NICE in the UK, FIGO internationally, the RCOG, and the WHO. They cover the same conditions, but their thresholds and their timing advice sometimes differ. A naive system trained or prompted on all of them will quietly pick one answer and hide the fact that there was any disagreement at all. The clinician never learns there was a choice to make. Later in the talk I will show a real, well-documented example of this with gestational diabetes thresholds. For now, the point is that handling disagreement openly is part of being trustworthy.

I use obstetrics as the test case, and it is a demanding one. The field is guideline-dense: NICE, FIGO, RCOG, and WHO all publish overlapping recommendations. Many decisions are time-critical, and you are always caring for two patients at the same time, the mother and the baby. Here is the kind of question a clinician actually asks at the bedside. Thirty-six weeks pregnant, blood pressure 160 over 110, severe headache, already on labetalol. What do I do now? A good system has to recognise this as possible severe pre-eclampsia, pull the right recommendations, flag how urgent it is, and show where every statement comes from. Each method is strong exactly where the other is weak. So why choose?

To ground the model we first have to retrieve the right knowledge, and there are two main ways to do it. The first is vector search. We turn text into numbers that capture meaning, and we find passages that are semantically similar to the question. This is excellent for simple lookups, for example a drug dose, even when the wording differs. The second is graph traversal. Here knowledge is stored as concepts linked by explicit relationships, and we follow those links. This is what you need for multi-step reasoning, for example: this condition escalates to that risk, which requires this monitoring. Vector search struggles with that chain of logic. Graph traversal struggles if no structured graph exists yet. The key insight of this whole talk is that each method is strong exactly where the other is weak. So why choose?

This is the core of the state of the art, and the message is consistent across very different groups. Ali and colleagues grounded an LLM in an ontology-based knowledge graph and cut hallucinations from 63 percent down to under 2 percent, with accuracy jumping from 37 to 98 percent. Buonocore and colleagues, in rheumatology, combined a SNOMED-CT graph with vector search and fused the two. For simple questions vectors alone were fine, but for complex, multi-step cases the hybrid was far better, with ranking quality going from about 0.44 to 0.77 and the correct answer appearing in the top five every single time. Thio and colleagues built a hybrid graph-and-vector system on real patient records and went from about 52 percent to 100 percent accuracy on multi-hop questions, with zero unsafe answers. Gao's team fed graph reasoning paths into the model and improved accuracy while experts flagged no unsafe outputs. And the HALO framework improved accuracy by up to 27 points just with smarter retrieval. Different diseases, same conclusion: combine structure and semantics, and you get both grounding and safety.

There is a prerequisite that is easy to overlook. Guidelines today are mostly long narrative documents written for humans. A computer cannot reason over a PDF. Chehab and colleagues, in a recent extension of the well-known GIN-McMaster checklist, describe how guidelines move from narrative text, to semi-structured tagged concepts, to fully structured data such as decision trees, and finally to executable artefacts that run inside software, often using the FHIR standard. This progression, from L1 to L4, is exactly the bridge we need. My knowledge graph is one concrete way of climbing those levels for obstetric guidelines. So this sets up why we build a structured obstetric ontology in the first place.

The gap: this has barely reached obstetrics

Graph-RAG is almost absent in obstetric decision support

No obstetric ontology or knowledge graph exists

So the evidence is strong, but here is the gap. Almost all of this work is in other specialties: COVID data, rheumatology, hepatology, general records. Graph-RAG has barely reached obstetrics. There is no obstetric-specific knowledge graph or ontology that I could find. And crucially, multi-step reasoning is rarely evaluated with real clinicians rather than automatic scores. That is a clear, unmet opportunity, and the rest of the talk is about how to take it.

ObstetricRAG

Our proposal, one stage at a time.

ObstetricRAG: one question, two retrievers, one grounded answer

The two retrievers run in parallel, then merge.

The model can only use what was retrieved.

Here is the proposed system, called ObstetricRAG. It takes the clinician's plain-language question and runs it through eight stages. First we parse the question and do entity linking, mapping phrases like "high blood pressure" to standard codes in SNOMED-CT and ICD-11. Then we classify the intent: is this a simple fact, a multi-step question, an urgent case, a possible contradiction, or a dose lookup? That routing matters. Next, and this is the heart of it, two retrievers run in parallel. The graph traversal walks the obstetric knowledge graph in Neo4j to follow clinical relationships. At the same time, vector search finds semantically similar guideline passages. We then merge the two result lists using reciprocal rank fusion, a simple and robust way to combine rankings, and we rerank the best candidates. Only then does the language model write the answer, and it can only use what was retrieved, with citations and safety flags attached. So the model is a careful writer, not the source of truth.

Now the four stages of ObstetricRAG, one at a time. Stage one is understanding the question. Three things happen. First, entity linking: the free text is mapped to standard codes in SNOMED-CT and ICD-11, so "high blood pressure at 36 weeks with a headache" becomes recognised concepts. Second, intent classification: the system decides this is a multi-hop, urgent, treatment-and-escalation question, not a simple fact. That routing decides how hard the retrieval works. Third, query rewriting: it writes a clean, well-formed version of the question to improve search recall. The payoff is that standard words become standard codes, and the system already knows this is urgent and multi-step.

RRF score = 1 / (k + rank)

Fusion does not care where a result came from, only how highly each source ranked it. The reranker then reads each candidate against the question and keeps the most relevant, ready for the model.

Stage three combines the two result lists. We use reciprocal rank fusion, a simple and robust method: each candidate gets a score of one over k plus its rank, and we add up the scores from both lists. It does not care whether a result came from the graph or the vectors, only how highly each ranked it. Then a cross-encoder reranker reads every surviving candidate against the actual question and reorders them by true relevance, which is more accurate than the first-pass search. What comes out is a small, high-quality set of evidence, each item still carrying its provenance, ready to hand to the model.

Stage four is generation, and the model is kept on a short leash. Its instructions are strict: use only the retrieved evidence, never outside knowledge; cite the source for every claim; and explicitly say so when the evidence is weak or the guidelines conflict. Just as important, the model returns a structured output, not free text: a fixed shape with the answer, the list of citations, the escalation flags, a confidence value, and an uncertainty note. Because those are guaranteed fields, the citations and the safety flags are always present by construction, not something we hope the model remembered to include. This is what makes the next part, trustworthiness, possible.

Three design choices for trust

  • Aligned to SNOMED-CT and ICD-11
  • Provenance on every edge (source, section, version)
  • Explicit vs inferred edges kept separate

Example: our pre-eclampsia case

The graph is the piece that does not exist yet for obstetrics, so building it is a contribution in itself. Nodes are things like conditions, drugs, thresholds, risk factors, protocols, and the text chunks themselves. Edges are clinical relationships: a drug indicates a condition, a condition escalates to a risk, a drug is contraindicated by something, a treatment has a dose, and a newer guideline supersedes an older one. Three design choices make it trustworthy. First, every concept is aligned to SNOMED-CT and ICD-11, so different wordings map to the same code. Second, every edge carries its provenance: which guideline, which section, which version. That is what makes citation automatic rather than an afterthought. Third, we keep explicit edges, what the guideline actually states, separate from inferred edges, what we deduced. Going back to our case, the graph knows that severe pre-eclampsia can escalate to impending eclampsia, which requires specific monitoring.

Trust is not a single feature, so we build it in three ways. First, every answer carries inline citations that point to the exact guideline and section, so the clinician can verify each claim. Second, escalation flags. As we walk the graph, certain nodes are marked red or amber, so if a case involves, say, delivery within 24 to 48 hours, that urgency is surfaced automatically and prominently. These flags come from the graph structure, so they are auditable, not just generated text. Third, contradiction detection. Here is a real example. For gestational diabetes, NICE uses a fasting glucose threshold of 5.6, while WHO and FIGO use 5.1. A patient at 5.3 is diabetic by one guideline and not by the other. A naive system picks one silently. Ours detects that the same concept has conflicting values from different sources, and it shows the clinician both, rather than hiding the disagreement. That is what trustworthy means in practice.

Looking ahead

Two open directions worth watching.

Where the field is heading: retrieval that checks itself

Standard RAG

retrieve once, then answer

Agentic RAG: retrieve, evaluate, refine

Self-correcting agentic graph-RAG, hepatology (2025): faithfulness 0.94 vs 0.87, expert safety 4.9/5 vs 4.4/5 for basic graph-RAG.

Related: Self-RAG, Corrective RAG, Microsoft GraphRAG.  Open question: should ObstetricRAG become iterative and self-checking for urgent multi-step cases?

I want to be honest that the field is moving fast, so let me end with two open directions. The first is agentic RAG. Standard RAG retrieves once and then writes. Agentic RAG turns retrieval into a loop: the system retrieves, evaluates whether it has enough, refines its own search, and only then answers. A 2025 self-correcting agentic graph-RAG in hepatology used exactly this retrieve, evaluate, refine loop and reached a faithfulness of 0.94 and an expert safety score of 4.9 out of 5, clearly above a basic graph-RAG. Related ideas like Self-RAG, Corrective RAG, and Microsoft's GraphRAG point the same way. So the open question for my work is whether ObstetricRAG should move from a single-shot pipeline to an iterative, self-checking one, especially for urgent multi-step questions where a second look really matters.

Also still open:  prospective clinical validationguideline and model driftmultilingual and local adaptation

The second open direction is about the knowledge itself. How should a guideline be stored so both clinicians and machines can use it? The established answer is to encode it as a FHIR artefact, the executable level of that ladder I showed earlier. It is rigorous but heavy to author. A very new alternative is the Open Knowledge Format, published by Google Cloud just last month, which stores knowledge as plain markdown files with a little structured header, readable by a person and parseable by an AI agent at the same time. My open question is whether obstetric guidelines could be curated in an OKF-style layer that feeds the graph, keeping the provenance and version history human-readable. And a few things stay genuinely open for all of us: proving benefit in a real prospective clinical study, handling guidelines and models that drift over time, and adapting to other languages and local protocols.
